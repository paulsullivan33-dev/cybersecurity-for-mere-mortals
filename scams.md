# Scams That Target Regular People

**The one-sentence version:** Nearly every scam works the same
way — manufactured urgency plus a request to act *now*, before
you can think or verify. Slow down, and the scam falls apart.

Every scam below follows the same three-part shape: **the hook**
(what grabs you), **the tell** (what gives it away), and **the
one-line response** (what to do). For the techniques behind these,
see [Phishing and Social Engineering](phishing.md).

## Tech-support popups

- **The hook:** A full-screen browser popup claims your computer
  is infected and gives a phone number for "Microsoft support."
- **The tell:** Real companies never announce infections through
  browser popups. The popup often plays an alarm sound and won't
  let you close the tab normally.
- **The one-line response:** Close the browser (use the task
  manager / force-quit if you must) and never call the number.

## "Your account is locked" texts

- **The hook:** A text says your bank, email, or package-delivery
  account is locked or suspended, with a link to fix it.
- **The tell:** The link doesn't go to the real site (hover or
  long-press to see the address), and the message demands
  *immediate* action.
- **The one-line response:** Delete the text; check your account
  by opening the app yourself or typing the address you know.

## Fake invoice / CEO email

- **The hook:** An email that looks like it's from your boss (or
  a vendor) asks you to pay an invoice or buy gift cards
  urgently.
- **The tell:** The sender's actual address is slightly wrong,
  the request is unusual for that person, and it always needs to
  happen *today*.
- **The one-line response:** Confirm through a separate channel
  you already trust — walk to their desk, call their known
  number.

## QR code swaps

- **The hook:** A sticker with a QR code is placed over the real
  one on a parking meter, restaurant table, or flyer, sending
  you to a fake payment or login page.
- **The tell:** The code is a sticker over something printed, the
  site asks for login or card details for a simple task, and the
  address looks wrong.
- **The one-line response:** Peel suspicious stickers aside and
  compare; type the address yourself instead of scanning.

## Romance / crypto "pig-butchering"

- **The hook:** A stranger befriends you online (dating app,
  social media, wrong-number text) and, after weeks of friendly
  chat, introduces a "can't-miss" crypto investment.
- **The tell:** They steer you to an unfamiliar trading site or
  app, early small "profits" build trust, and withdrawals later
  require extra fees or are refused.
- **The one-line response:** No real romance or friend needs you
  to invest — stop sending money and cut contact.

## The grandparent scam

- **The hook:** A caller claims to be your grandchild (or a
  police officer / lawyer calling about them), in trouble and
  needing money wired *right now*, and begs you not to tell
  anyone.
- **The tell:** The secrecy, the urgency, and the payment method
  — wire transfers, gift cards, or crypto are scammer favorites
  because they can't be reversed.
- **The one-line response:** Hang up and call the grandchild (or
  the parents) on a number you already have.

## Verification habits that beat all of them

1. **Call back on a known number** — never the one they gave
   you. The number on your bank card, the company's real site, or
   the contact already in your phone.
2. **Break the urgency.** Any demand for *right now* is the
   scam's engine. Waiting an hour costs you nothing and costs
   the scammer everything.
3. **Never pay in gift cards, wire transfers, or crypto** at a
   stranger's request. Legitimate organizations don't ask for
   those.
4. **Type addresses yourself.** Links in messages and QR codes
   are how you end up on a lookalike site.
